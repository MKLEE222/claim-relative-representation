# Editorial boundaries and the authority to correct

*Draft source-analysis section. Retrospective close readings and a structural audit; no whole-book prevalence or proof of representation insufficiency.*

The unit of editorial intervention cannot be recovered reliably from one heading syntax. In the examined digital witness, a page-only erratum, an upper-case page marker, and an indented quotation introduce distinct interventions without conforming to the earlier extraction rule. Their importance is not exhausted by counting missed entries. The short instruction on page 50 of Cordier's Addenda replaces *found* with *founded* in the 1903 note on Urumtsi. The earlier printed sentence describes finding a town, whereas the correction specifies founding one. A seemingly small textual amendment therefore changes the kind of historical action described (Yule-Cordier 1903, I, p.201; Cordier 1920, p.50). The evidence establishes the amendment, not the event's historical truth. Its attachment to a preceding discussion of desert sounds in the derived frame would obscure which proposition was being corrected.

Boundaries also delimit responsibility. At page 110, Cordier's transmission of Laufer's discussion of dog-headed peoples is followed by an indented Ceylon passage, with a separate chapter-page heading, and a comparison from Chau Ju-kwa. The latter entry juxtaposes descriptions of a royal jewel and clothing; it does not explicitly certify every detail or establish the identity of the jewels. Reading the merged digital interval as a single act risks confusing both the topic and the responsible voice. This does not show that an attentive reader cannot recover the distinction from plain text. It shows that the research inventory must not erase that distinction before testing recoverability.

The Supplementary Note on the Andaman and Nicobar Islands provides a more demanding case. It is an extended signed contribution rather than one homogeneous update. Cordier introduces notes sent by Richard C. Temple; Temple subsequently identifies himself through his administrative role and publications, invokes the Census Report as a comprehensive source, and recommends recasting several earlier notes. The recommendation is part of the historical object: it must not be treated as evidence that a fourth edition actually incorporated the proposed changes. The signature dated 29 November 1919 supplies a further temporal boundary distinct from publication in the 1920 collection (Cordier 1920, pp.144-145, 150).

Within that contribution, the relevant relation depends on the earlier layer being addressed. Temple rejects the cannibalism allegation, but the earlier digital witness distinguishes the translated narrative, where that allegation appears, from the commentary, where its general rejection is already recorded. His denial contradicts the narrative allegation while continuing an existing source-critical position. This comparison remains qualified by its verification asymmetry: the later printed page was inspected, whereas the earlier Volume II comparison was made against the frozen digital witness. It does not establish the first historical rejection of the allegation (Cordier 1920, p.149; Yule-Cordier 1903 digital witness, II, chapter XIII and note 1).

The same subnote differentiates the certainty of rejection from the grounds offered for explaining the allegation's origin. Temple's denial is categorical; the self-quoted account of the report's formation is introduced modally. His navigation note similarly affirms a proposition about coastal travel while doubting a different reported journey. These are not alternative labels for one update, but different propositions with different stated evidential commitments. The source also situates this corrective work within an administrative account of expertise and its inherited categories. Recognizing a correction need not endorse that authority claim or turn the collection into a simple narrative of epistemic progress.

The boundary audit therefore belongs inside, rather than before, the humanities argument. A heading-based extraction omitted some interventions, merged others, and allowed the final candidates to absorb separate bibliography and index sections as well as modern electronic paratext. The previous 223-entry object remains reproducible, but its numerical stability does not certify it as the complete or uniform historical population. A defensible inventory must distinguish publication sections, editorial contributions, page-addressed subnotes, quoted propositions, their responsible actors, and the evidential grounds asserted for them. Whether a transformed representation subsequently preserves these distinctions is a separate empirical test. The historical model cannot acquire its own losses and then attribute them to the representation under evaluation.
